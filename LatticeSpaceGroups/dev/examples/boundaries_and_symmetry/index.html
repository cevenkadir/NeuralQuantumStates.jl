Boundary conditions and symmetry

Boundary conditions are not a detail you set and forget: they decide which symmetries a finite lattice actually has. A periodic direction carries translation symmetry, an open one does not, and a mirror that sits at the origin on one lattice sits at the midpoint on another. All of that is worked out from the geometry, but it is worth seeing explicitly.

using LatticeSpaceGroups

Translations need periodicity

An open chain has no translation symmetry at all — shifting it moves sites off the end:

open_chain = build(Hypercube([6]; periodic=false))
translation_generators(open_chain)
Translation{1, Int64}[]

Close it into a ring and the generator appears:

ring = build(Hypercube([6]; periodic=true))
translation_generators(ring)
1-element Vector{Translation{1, Int64}}:
 Translation{1, Int64}([1])
translation_permutation(ring, 1)
6-element Vector{Int64}:
 2
 3
 4
 5
 6
 1

Asking for a translation along an open direction is an error rather than a wrong answer:

try
    translation_permutation(open_chain, 1)
catch err
    err
end
ArgumentError("axis 1 is not periodic, so it admits no translation symmetry")

Mixed boundaries: the cylinder

Periodicity is per dimension, so a 4 × 4 lattice periodic in one direction only is a cylinder. It has translation symmetry around the circumference and none along the axis:

cylinder = build(Hypercube([4, 4]; periodic=[true, false]))

length(translation_generators(cylinder)), length(translation_group(cylinder))
(1, 4)
is_symmetry(cylinder, Translation([1, 0])), is_symmetry(cylinder, Translation([0, 1]))
(true, false)

Compare the torus, where the translation group is the product of both cyclic groups:

torus = build(Hypercube([4, 4]; periodic=true))
length(translation_group(torus))
16

The mirror is not always at the origin

Reflections survive open boundaries, but the mirror plane moves. On an open chain it sits at the midpoint, so the permutation is the reversal:

reflection_permutation(open_chain, 1)
6-element Vector{Int64}:
 6
 5
 4
 3
 2
 1

Nothing about that was hard-coded. The reflection about the origin alone is not a symmetry — it sends site 1 to a position where no site exists — and compensating_translation finds the shift that repairs it:

mirror = [-1.0 ;;]

is_symmetry(open_chain, PointOperation(mirror))
false
compensating_translation(open_chain, mirror)
1-element StaticArraysCore.SVector{1, Float64} with indices SOneTo(1):
 5.0

That is the same mechanism that gives honeycomb and diamond their full point groups, where the symmetry centre is fixed by the site offsets rather than by the boundary.

Point groups survive opening the boundary

Opening the boundary costs translations, but not necessarily the point group. Both the periodic and the open square lattice have $D_4$, of order 8:

length(point_group(torus)), length(point_group(build(Hypercube([4, 4]; periodic=false))))
(8, 8)

The reflection is its own inverse in both cases, as a reflection should be:

compose(b, a) = [b[a[i]] for i in eachindex(a)]

p = reflection_permutation(torus, 1)
compose(p, p) == collect(1:n_sites(torus))
true

Why preserves_edges exists

A permutation can move the sites correctly and still not be a symmetry of anything you would build on the lattice, because it maps a bond onto a non-bond. Every operation this package returns is checked against the edge set, not just the site set:

all(preserves_edges(cylinder, site_permutation(cylinder, op)) for op in space_group(cylinder))
true

That check is on by default; pass check_edges=false to point_group or space_group if you genuinely want site-set symmetries only.
