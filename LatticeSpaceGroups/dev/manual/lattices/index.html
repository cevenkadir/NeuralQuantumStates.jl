Lattices

using LatticeSpaceGroups

Why not just a graph?

A graph gives you vertices and edges. A quantum lattice needs more:

  • Positions and a Bravais basis, so that "next-nearest neighbour" means something.
  • A periodic-boundary-aware metric, so neighbour orders are well defined on a torus rather than being cut by the boundary.
  • Space groups, which are the whole point: SymBasis.Translational, SymBasis.SpatialReflection, and SymBasis.Rotational each need a site permutation, and writing one by hand only ever works for a one-dimensional chain. See Symmetries.

A Lattice is a plain struct holding its sites and bonds, so none of this costs a graph dependency. If you want a graph, see Converting to a graph below.

Predefined lattices

A lattice is described by a spec, and build turns a spec into a Lattice. Specs validate on construction, so a mistake is caught where you wrote it rather than deep inside the build.

Hypercubic, and its named cases

Square and Cube are shorthands for the two commonest Hypercube shapes. A one-dimensional chain is Hypercube([n]) — there is deliberately no exported Chain, because that name collides with Lux.Chain and Flux.Chain, and this package is meant to be loaded alongside them.

build(Hypercube([7], 2.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,1,1}([7]; periodic=Bool[1], 7 sites, 7 bonds)

periodic may be one flag per dimension, which is how you get a cylinder or a slab:

build(Hypercube([3, 4, 5], 2.0; periodic=[false, true, false]))
Lattice{Float64,3,1}([3, 4, 5]; periodic=Bool[0, 1, 0], 60 sites, 148 bonds)

Triclinic

The least symmetric Bravais lattice: three independent edge lengths at three independent angles, given in degrees.

build(Triclinic([3, 2, 1], [1.0, 1.5, 2.0], [40.0, 65.0, 90.0]; periodic=false))
Lattice{Float64,3,1}([3, 2, 1]; periodic=Bool[0, 0, 0], 6 sites, 7 bonds)

Two-dimensional lattices

build(Triangular([2, 4], 1.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,2,1}([2, 4]; periodic=Bool[1, 1], 8 sites, 20 bonds)
build(Honeycomb([3, 6], 2.5; periodic=false))
Lattice{Float64,2,2}([3, 6]; periodic=Bool[0, 0], 36 sites, 45 bonds)
build(Kagome([4, 2], 3.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,2,3}([4, 2]; periodic=Bool[1, 1], 24 sites, 48 bonds)

For Honeycomb and Kagome, edge_length is the length of the primitive vectors, not the bond length — the sublattice offsets place the bonds at edge_length / √3 and edge_length / 2 respectively.

Three-dimensional lattices

BCC and FCC are built in the primitive basis, so shape counts primitive cells: an FCC([3, 3, 3]) has 27 sites, not the 108 of 27 conventional cubic cells. Their edge_length is the conventional cubic cell parameter a.

build(BCC([3, 3, 3], 1.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,3,1}([3, 3, 3]; periodic=Bool[1, 1, 1], 27 sites, 108 bonds)
build(FCC([3, 3, 3], 1.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,3,1}([3, 3, 3]; periodic=Bool[1, 1, 1], 27 sites, 162 bonds)

Diamond and Pyrochlore put a two- and four-site basis on the FCC lattice:

build(Diamond([2, 2, 2], 1.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,3,2}([2, 2, 2]; periodic=Bool[1, 1, 1], 16 sites, 32 bonds)
build(Pyrochlore([2, 2, 2], 1.0; periodic=true))
Lattice{Float64,3,4}([2, 2, 2]; periodic=Bool[1, 1, 1], 32 sites, 96 bonds)

Sites and bonds

Sites are numbered with the sublattice index varying fastest, then the first cell coordinate, and so on. That numbering is the lattice's interface to everything else — it is what site_permutation permutes and what a Hilbert space assigns its degrees of freedom to.

lat = build(Honeycomb([2, 2], 1.0; periodic=true))
site_labels(lat)

Further neighbour shells

max_order includes more than the nearest neighbours. bonds can then select a shell.

square = build(Square(4, 1.0; periodic=true); max_order=2)
length(bonds(square; order=1)), length(bonds(square; order=2))
(32, 32)

The second shell of a square lattice is its cell diagonals — which is why max_order is not simply "more grid steps".

Building a lattice by hand

For a lattice this package does not predefine, give a LatticeBasis its primitive vectors (one per column) and its site offsets:

basis_vectors = [
    1.0 0.25
    0.1 0.4
]
site_offsets = [
    0.0 0.0
    0.15 0.20
]
lat_basis = LatticeBasis(basis_vectors, site_offsets)
LatticeBasis{Float64, 2, 2}([1.0 0.25; 0.1 0.4], [0.0 0.0; 0.15 0.2])

The primitive vectors must be linearly independent; a singular set describes no two-dimensional lattice and is rejected.

Bonds from neighbour shells

Lattice([4, 3], lat_basis, [true, true]; max_order=1)
Lattice{Float64,2,2}([4, 3]; periodic=Bool[1, 1], 24 sites, 12 bonds)

Boundary conditions are per dimension, so a cylinder is [true, false]:

Lattice([4, 3], lat_basis, [true, false]; max_order=1)
Lattice{Float64,2,2}([4, 3]; periodic=Bool[1, 0], 24 sites, 12 bonds)

Bonds given explicitly

When the connectivity is not distance-derived, pass the bonds as site-index pairs. The orders keyword records which shell each belongs to, if that distinction matters to you.

Lattice([4, 3], lat_basis, [(1, 8), (2, 15)], [true, true])
Lattice{Float64,2,2}([4, 3]; periodic=Bool[1, 1], 24 sites, 2 bonds)

Converting to a graph

Loading Graphs.jl or MetaGraphsNext.jl activates an extension that converts a lattice. A MetaGraph carries the geometry a SimpleGraph has nowhere to put:

using LatticeSpaceGroups, MetaGraphsNext

mg = MetaGraph(build(Honeycomb([3, 3], 1.0; periodic=true)))
mg[(1, 1, 1)]                # position of sublattice 1 in cell (1, 1)
mg[(1, 1, 1), (2, 1, 1)]     # 1, a nearest-neighbour bond
